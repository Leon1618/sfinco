# Your Apple ID and iCloud


Protecting the master key to your digital life

If your iPhone is a house, then your Apple ID is the master key. Not just to the front door — to every room, every cupboard, and every safe inside. It controls your iCloud account, your photos, your backups, your App Store purchases, your iMessage history, your contacts, and Find My iPhone.

It is also the account that scammers most want to get their hands on — because if they have your Apple ID, they effectively have your phone.

This chapter covers how to check whether your Apple ID is properly secured, how to set up two-factor authentication if you haven't already, and what to do in the scenarios that frighten people most — being locked out, or suspecting someone else has access.

He lost access to his Apple ID for eleven days. Recovering it required a lengthy identity verification process with Apple Support. Two-factor authentication would have stopped this entirely — and it takes four minutes to set up.

## What is your Apple ID, exactly?


Your Apple ID is the email address and password you use to sign in to Apple services. It is usually the email address you used when you first set up your iPhone — often a Gmail, Hotmail, or iCloud address.

To see what Apple ID is currently signed in on your phone, look at the very top of Settings. You will see your name and, underneath it, your Apple ID email address.

Settings path:  Settings  >  [Your Name at the top]

*[ SCREENSHOT: Top of Settings screen showing name and Apple ID email address ]*



Your Apple ID is shown at the very top of Settings, just below your name. Tap it to see everything connected to your account.

Tapping your name opens your Apple ID page, where you can see every device signed in to your account, your iCloud storage, and your payment methods. We will come back to this page several times in this chapter.


## The password — and why it matters more than any other


Your Apple ID password is the most important password on your phone. If it is weak, reused from another account, or written on a sticky note on your fridge, everything else in this book is undermined.

What makes a strong Apple ID password?

At least 12 characters long

A mix of uppercase and lowercase letters, numbers, and at least one symbol

Not a word from the dictionary — even with letter substitutions like '3' for 'e'

Not your name, your pet's name, your birth year, or your address

Not used for any other account — especially not your email or banking

> **★ TIP**
>
> The strongest passwords are passphrases — three or four random words joined together, with a number and symbol added. For example: 'Mango!Rainfall47Desk' is extremely strong, completely unique, and far easier to remember than a random string of characters. Pick words that have no connection to each other or to you personally.



How to change your Apple ID password

Settings path:  Settings  >  [Your Name]  >  Sign-In & Security  >  Change Password

Open Settings  and tap your name at the top.

Tap Sign-In & Security.

Tap Change Password.

You may be asked to enter your iPhone passcode first.

Enter your current Apple ID password,  then type and confirm your new one.

Tap Change  to save.

*[ SCREENSHOT: Settings > [Name] > Sign-In & Security screen showing Change Password option ]*



The Sign-In & Security screen under your Apple ID settings. This is also where you manage two-factor authentication and trusted phone numbers.

> **⚠ WARNING**
>
> After changing your Apple ID password, you will be signed out of iCloud on all other devices. This is normal. You will need to sign back in on your iPad, Mac, or other Apple devices using your new password. Have those devices nearby before you make the change.




## Two-factor authentication — your single most important security step


Two-factor authentication — often written as 2FA — means that signing in to your Apple ID requires two things: your password, and a six-digit code sent to a trusted device or phone number that only you have access to.

Even if a scammer somehow obtains your Apple ID password, they cannot access your account without that code. And since the code arrives on your iPhone or is sent to your phone number, they would need to physically have your device to intercept it.

This one feature stops the vast majority of account takeover attacks. It is not optional — it is essential.

> **ℹ DID YOU KNOW?**
>
> Apple now requires two-factor authentication for most Apple ID accounts created after 2019. However, many older accounts still do not have it enabled — particularly those created when iPhones first became popular. If your account is more than five years old, there is a good chance 2FA is not yet active. Check right now using the steps below.



How to check if two-factor authentication is already on

Settings path:  Settings  >  [Your Name]  >  Sign-In & Security

Look for the line that says Two-Factor Authentication. If it says On next to it, you are already protected. If it says Off, or if you do not see this option at all, follow the steps below to turn it on.

*[ SCREENSHOT: Sign-In & Security screen showing Two-Factor Authentication: On ]*



The Sign-In & Security screen. 'Two-Factor Authentication: On' means you are protected. If it shows 'Off', follow the steps in the next section immediately.

How to turn on two-factor authentication

Open Settings  and tap your name at the top.

Tap Sign-In & Security.

Tap Turn On Two-Factor Authentication.

Tap Continue.

Enter a trusted phone number  — this is the number where Apple will send verification codes. Use your mobile number.

Choose how to receive codes:  Text Message is the simplest option for most people.

Apple will send a six-digit code to that number.  Enter it to confirm.

Two-factor authentication is now active.

*[ SCREENSHOT: Two-Factor Authentication setup screen showing trusted phone number entry ]*



Enter your mobile number as the trusted phone number. Apple will send a six-digit code here whenever someone tries to sign in to your Apple ID from a new device.

> **✓ WELL DONE IF YOU HAVE THIS**
>
> Two-factor authentication is now on. From this point forward, anyone who tries to sign in to your Apple ID — including you, on a new device — will need both your password and a six-digit code sent to your phone. This closes the single most commonly exploited vulnerability in Apple account security.




## Trusted phone numbers and trusted devices


Two-factor authentication works by sending a code to a trusted device or trusted phone number. It is worth understanding what these are and making sure yours are up to date.

Trusted devices

A trusted device is any Apple device — iPhone, iPad, or Mac — that is already signed in to your Apple ID and has two-factor authentication turned on. When you sign in to a new device or browser, a notification appears on your trusted devices showing the sign-in location and a six-digit code.

Trusted phone numbers

A trusted phone number is a mobile number that can receive SMS codes. You should have at least one, and ideally two — your own mobile number, and perhaps a family member's number as a backup in case your phone is lost or unavailable.

Settings path:  Settings  >  [Your Name]  >  Sign-In & Security  >  Trusted Phone Number

Tap Add a Trusted Phone Number  to add a second number.

Enter the number  and choose verification method (Text Message recommended).

Enter the code sent to that number  to confirm.

> **★ TIP**
>
> Adding a trusted family member's number as a backup is a smart move — particularly if you have an adult child or trusted person who could help you recover your account if your phone was ever lost or stolen. Make sure they know why their number is listed and that they should always tell you if they receive an Apple verification code — they should never share it with anyone else, including people claiming to be from Apple.




## iCloud — what it is and why it matters


iCloud is Apple's cloud storage service — a secure online space where your iPhone automatically saves copies of your photos, contacts, messages, notes, and app data. It is what allows you to switch to a new iPhone and have everything just appear on the new device as if nothing changed.

iCloud is tied entirely to your Apple ID. Which means that protecting your Apple ID is also protecting your iCloud — they are the same thing.

What is backed up to iCloud?

By default, iCloud backs up:

Your photos and videos

Your contacts, calendars, and reminders

Your text messages and iMessage history

Your app data and settings

Your Safari bookmarks and history

Your health data

Your iPhone settings and home screen layout

This means that if your phone is lost, stolen, or damaged, you can restore everything to a new phone from iCloud. It is one of the most valuable features of the iPhone ecosystem — but only if it is turned on and working correctly.

How to check your iCloud backup is working

Settings path:  Settings  >  [Your Name]  >  iCloud  >  iCloud Backup

Open Settings  and tap your name.

Tap iCloud.

Scroll down and tap iCloud Backup.

Make sure iCloud Backup is toggled ON  (green).

Check the line that says Last Successful Backup.  If this was more than a week ago, tap Back Up Now to run a manual backup.

*[ SCREENSHOT: Settings > iCloud > iCloud Backup screen showing backup toggle ON and last backup date/time ]*



The iCloud Backup screen. The toggle should be green (on), and the 'Last Successful Backup' date should be recent — ideally within the last 24 hours if you charge your phone overnight.

> **ℹ DID YOU KNOW?**
>
> iCloud backup runs automatically when your iPhone is plugged in to power, connected to Wi-Fi, and the screen is locked — typically overnight while you sleep. If you charge your phone beside your bed, your backup is almost certainly running every night without you needing to do anything.




## Reviewing devices signed in to your Apple ID


This is something most people have never done — and it only takes a minute. You can see every device currently signed in to your Apple ID, and remove any that should not be there.

This is important because if a family member once borrowed your Apple ID to download an app years ago, or if you have an old device that was never properly signed out, those devices still have access to your account.

Settings path:  Settings  >  [Your Name]  >  scroll down to see device list

Scroll to the bottom of your Apple ID page. You will see a list of every Apple device signed in with your account. Tap any device to see its model, iOS version, and whether it is a trusted device for two-factor authentication.

If you see a device you do not recognise — or an old device you no longer own — tap it and select Remove from Account.

*[ SCREENSHOT: Apple ID page scrolled down showing list of trusted devices — iPhone, iPad, MacBook ]*



Your list of signed-in devices is at the bottom of your Apple ID page. Tap any device to see its details, or to remove it from your account if it should not be there.

> **⚠ WARNING**
>
> If you see a device on this list that you do not recognise and do not own, remove it immediately, then change your Apple ID password and contact Apple Support at support.apple.com. An unrecognised device is a strong signal that someone else may have access to your account.




## What to do if you are locked out of your Apple ID


Being locked out of your Apple ID is one of the most stressful digital experiences there is. It can happen for several reasons — too many incorrect password attempts, a forgotten password, or losing access to your trusted phone number.

Here is what to do, in order.

Option 1 — Reset your password using a trusted device

If you have another Apple device signed in to your account — an iPad or Mac — you can reset your Apple ID password directly from that device without needing to contact Apple.

Settings path:  On another Apple device:  Settings  >  [Your Name]  >  Sign-In & Security  >  Change Password

Option 2 — Use account recovery at iforgot.apple.com

Go to iforgot.apple.com on any web browser. Enter your Apple ID email address and follow the prompts. Apple will send a verification code to your trusted phone number or trusted device.

Option 3 — Account Recovery Contact

Apple allows you to nominate an Account Recovery Contact — a trusted person who can help you regain access to your account if you are locked out. This person does not get access to your account; they simply receive a code that you use during the recovery process.

Settings path:  Settings  >  [Your Name]  >  Sign-In & Security  >  Account Recovery

> **★ TIP**
>
> Setting up an Account Recovery Contact now — before you ever need it — takes about two minutes and could save you days of frustration if you are ever locked out. Choose a trusted adult family member with their own Apple device. They will receive a request to accept the role and can decline if they prefer — their number is not added without their confirmation.



> **✶ CRITICAL — READ THIS CAREFULLY**
>
> Never share your Apple ID verification code with anyone — not even someone claiming to be from Apple Support. Apple will never call you and ask for a verification code. If someone calls you and says they are from Apple and need your code to help you, hang up immediately. This is one of the most common and effective scams targeting iPhone users in Australia. Your code is yours alone — sharing it hands over your account.

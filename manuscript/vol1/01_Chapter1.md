# Why your iPhone is a target


What scammers are after — and how they get in

> **▶ REAL STORY**
>
> Diane, 71, from the Sunshine Coast, received a text message from what appeared to be her bank. It said her account had been flagged for suspicious activity and she needed to verify her details immediately. The link looked right. The logo looked right. She clicked it, entered her name, date of birth, and internet banking password. Within four hours, $14,000 had been transferred out of her account. Diane is not careless. She is intelligent, experienced, and careful with her money. She was targeted by professionals — and she had no idea what to look for.



Stories like Diane's are not rare. They happen every day across Australia — to people of all ages, all backgrounds, and all levels of technical experience. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission reported over $3.1 billion lost to scams in Australia in 2022 alone. That figure has grown every year since.

And the iPhone — one of the most popular and trusted devices in the world — sits right at the centre of it all.

This chapter is not here to frighten you. It is here to show you clearly what is actually happening, why your phone is valuable to the people who want to misuse it, and what the most common methods of attack look like. Because once you know what to look for, most of these threats become remarkably easy to avoid.


## What is actually on your iPhone?


To understand why your iPhone is worth targeting, it helps to think about what is stored on it. Probably far more than you realise.

Your bank accounts — through banking apps or saved browser sessions

Your email — which can be used to reset passwords for almost everything else

Your Apple ID — the master key to your Apple account, iCloud, purchases, and Find My

Your photos — including images of documents, Medicare cards, passports, and bank statements that many people photograph for convenience

Your contacts — names, phone numbers, and addresses of everyone you know

Your location history — everywhere you have been, often going back months or years

Your saved passwords — stored in Safari or third-party apps

Your text messages — including one-time codes sent by your bank for verification

Your health data — through Apple Health and connected apps

> **ℹ DID YOU KNOW?**
>
> A scammer who gains access to your iPhone — or even just your email account — can often reset your internet banking password, intercept the verification code sent to your phone, and drain your account, all within a few minutes. Your phone and your email together are sometimes called the 'keys to the kingdom.' That is not an exaggeration.



Most people think of their iPhone as a phone and a camera. Scammers think of it as a vault.


## How do attacks actually happen?


There is a common misconception that getting scammed or hacked requires the victim to do something obviously stupid — click a link that says "FREE MONEY" in flashing letters, or hand their phone to a stranger. The reality is very different.

Modern attacks are designed to be convincing. They are tested on thousands of people before they reach you. They exploit genuine moments of uncertainty, urgency, or helpfulness. Here are the most common methods used against iPhone users in Australia today.

Phishing — fake messages that look real

Phishing is the practice of sending a message — by text, email, or even voicemail — that impersonates a trusted organisation. The goal is to get you to click a link and enter your details on a fake website that looks identical to the real one.

In Australia, the most commonly impersonated organisations are:

The Australian Taxation Office (ATO) — "You have an outstanding tax debt"

myGov — "Your account has been suspended"

Australia Post — "Your parcel could not be delivered"

Telstra or Optus — "Your account has been compromised"

Your bank — "Unusual activity detected on your account"

Apple — "Your Apple ID has been locked"

> **⚠ WARNING**
>
> A genuine message from the ATO, your bank, or Apple will NEVER ask you to click a link and enter your password, PIN, or full account details. If a message asks for this, it is a scam. When in doubt, close the message, go directly to the official website by typing it yourself, or call the organisation on a number you find independently — not one in the message.



Vishing — scam phone calls

Vishing (voice phishing) involves a phone call, often from someone claiming to be from your bank, the police, the ATO, or even Apple support. The caller will usually tell you that something is urgently wrong — your account has been hacked, you owe a tax debt, your computer has a virus, or your identity has been stolen.

They will sound professional and confident. They may already know your name, your address, or even part of your account number (this information is sometimes purchased from data breaches). And they will use urgency and fear to push you into acting quickly, before you have time to think clearly.

> **▶ REAL STORY**
>
> A 68-year-old man in Brisbane received a call from someone claiming to be from the Commonwealth Bank fraud team. The caller knew his name, suburb, and the last four digits of his card. He was told his account had been compromised and he needed to transfer his savings to a 'safe account' immediately. He transferred $47,000 before a family member intervened. The bank cannot verify caller identity over the phone. Scammers exploit this completely.



Fake app updates and malicious apps

Occasionally, scammers create apps that look like legitimate tools — a utility, a game, a productivity app — but are designed to harvest your data or display fake alerts. Apple's App Store has strong protections against this, but it is not perfect.

More commonly, you might receive an email or pop-up message claiming that your iPhone needs a security update and directing you to download something. Apple never delivers updates this way. All genuine iPhone updates come through Settings > General > Software Update and nowhere else.

SIM swapping

SIM swapping is a more sophisticated attack in which a criminal contacts your mobile carrier — Telstra, Optus, or Vodafone — and convinces them to transfer your phone number to a new SIM card that the criminal controls.

Once they have your number, they can receive the SMS verification codes that your bank sends when you log in or make transfers. Combined with a stolen password, this gives them full access to your accounts.

We cover how to protect yourself against SIM swapping in Chapter 3.


## Why older Australians are targeted more


It is worth addressing this directly, because pretending otherwise would not help anyone.

People over 60 are disproportionately targeted by phone and online scams. This is not because older people are less intelligent or less capable. It is because of several factors that scammers deliberately exploit:

Higher average savings and superannuation balances make the potential reward greater

Less familiarity with how digital systems actually work makes deception easier

Greater tendency to be polite and engage with callers rather than hanging up

More time at home and more likely to answer calls and messages

Less likely to have discussed scam tactics with others or seen warnings on social media

> **ℹ DID YOU KNOW?**
>
> According to the ACCC's Scamwatch, Australians aged 65 and over lost more money to scams per capita than any other age group in 2022. Investment scams and remote access scams were the most costly. But the same data also shows that when older Australians are informed and aware, they report scam attempts at higher rates than younger Australians. Awareness is genuinely protective.



This guide was written with this in mind. Every step in the chapters that follow is chosen specifically because it addresses the most common and most damaging risks for everyday iPhone users in Australia. You do not need to become a cybersecurity expert. You just need to know enough to recognise the warning signs — and to have the right settings in place so that even if something does go wrong, the damage is limited.


## The good news


Here is what the scammers do not want you to know: most of these attacks have simple, effective defences.

A strong passcode. Two-factor authentication on your Apple ID. Knowing what a phishing message looks like. Having a backup of your phone. That is honestly most of it.

None of these things are complicated. None of them cost money. And none of them require you to become a technology expert. They just require a bit of time and the willingness to follow a few steps.

That is exactly what this book is for. Each chapter that follows will take one area of your iPhone's security, explain why it matters, and walk you through exactly what to do — step by step, with screenshots.

By the time you finish this book, your iPhone will be significantly more protected than it is right now. And more importantly, you will understand why — which means you will be able to make better decisions every time something suspicious comes your way.

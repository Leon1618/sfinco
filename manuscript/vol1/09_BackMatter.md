# Glossary

These are the key terms used throughout this book, explained in plain English. You do not need to memorise them — this section is here to help if you encounter a word and want a clear definition.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| 2FA / Two-Factor Authentication | A security method that requires two things to log in: your password, plus a code sent to your phone or device. Even if someone has your password, they cannot get in without the code. |
| Activation Lock | A feature that locks an iPhone to your Apple ID, making it impossible to erase or reactivate without your Apple ID password — even if the phone is factory reset. |
| Apple ID | The email address and password you use to sign in to Apple services — iCloud, the App Store, iMessage, FaceTime, and more. It is the master key to everything Apple. |
| App Privacy Report | An iOS feature that records which permissions your apps have accessed and which external servers they have contacted, giving you a log of actual app behaviour. |
| Encryption | The process of scrambling data so it can only be read by someone with the right key. HTTPS encrypts your web traffic; iCloud encrypts your backups. |
| Evil Twin | A fake Wi-Fi network set up to mimic a legitimate one. When your phone connects to it, the attacker can see your internet traffic. |
| Face ID | Apple's facial recognition system. It maps your face using 30,000+ infrared dots and uses that map to unlock your iPhone and authorise payments. |
| Find My | Apple's location and device tracking service. It lets you locate, lock, display a message on, or remotely erase your iPhone from another device. |
| HTTPS | The secure version of the web protocol. When a website address starts with https://, your connection to it is encrypted — shown as a padlock in your browser. |
| iCloud | Apple's cloud storage service. It backs up your photos, contacts, messages, and settings, and allows them to be restored to a new device. |
| IDCARE | Australia's national identity and cyber support service. Free to use. Call 1800 595 160 if you have been affected by a scam or identity theft. |
| IMEI | A unique 15-digit number that identifies your specific iPhone. Needed when reporting theft to police. Found at Settings > General > About > IMEI. |
| Malware | Malicious software — programs designed to damage, spy on, or gain unauthorised access to a device. Rare on iPhone but possible through compromised apps. |
| Medical ID | An iOS feature that stores your medical conditions, allergies, and emergency contacts, visible from your lock screen without needing your passcode. |
| Passcode | The PIN or password used to unlock your iPhone. Can be four digits, six digits, or a custom alphanumeric code. Underpins all other iPhone security. |
| Permission | An explicit grant of access from you to an app — for example, allowing an app to use your camera, microphone, or location. |
| Phishing | A scam that impersonates a trusted organisation — via SMS, email, or a fake website — to trick you into handing over personal or financial information. |
| Scamwatch | The Australian government's national scam reporting service, run by the ACCC. Visit scamwatch.gov.au to report scams or check current alerts. |
| SIM Swapping | An attack where a criminal convinces your mobile carrier to transfer your phone number to a SIM they control, giving them access to your SMS verification codes. |
| Stolen Device Protection | An iOS 17.3+ feature that requires Face ID (not just a passcode) to make sensitive account changes when your phone is away from trusted locations. |
| Tracking (App) | When an app follows your activity across other apps and websites to build an advertising profile. Requires your permission under iOS App Tracking Transparency. |
| VPN (Virtual Private Network) | A service that encrypts your internet traffic and routes it through a secure server. Particularly useful on public Wi-Fi to prevent interception. |
| Vishing | Voice phishing — a scam conducted by phone call rather than text or email, often impersonating banks, the ATO, or technology companies. |
| WPA2 / WPA3 | Wi-Fi security protocols that encrypt wireless network traffic. WPA3 is the current standard; WPA2 is also acceptable. Older protocols (WEP, WPA) are insecure. |

## If your phone goes missing — first 30 minutes

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Locate it | icloud.com/find on any browser, or Find My app |
| 2. Mark as Lost | In Find My — locks phone and displays your message |
| 3. Call your bank | To flag Apple Pay on the device |
| 4. Report to police | Provide IMEI and Find My location |
| 5. Erase if needed | Find My > Erase This Device (last resort) |

## If you receive a suspicious message or call

| Step | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Do not click any links | And do not call numbers in the message |
| 2. Find real contact details | Official website or card — not from the message |
| 3. Verify independently | Call the organisation using their real number |
| 4. Report it | scamwatch.gov.au and the impersonated organisation |

# About the author

The author of the Sfinco Guides series works in banking and technology on the Sunshine Coast in Queensland, with nearly two decades of experience across customer service, financial services, and digital security.

Holding qualifications in cybersecurity (CompTIA Security+), financial advising (RG146), and information technology, and completing further studies at CQUniversity, the author brings together a rare combination of technical knowledge and the ability to explain it clearly to people who have no interest in becoming technical experts.

The inspiration for this series came from years of seeing the same pattern: intelligent, capable, careful people being harmed by scams and digital threats — not because they were careless, but because nobody had ever given them a clear, practical guide to protecting themselves. That gap is what these books exist to close.

The author also volunteers with CoderDojo, teaching digital skills to young people on the Sunshine Coast, and has a background in community technology education across Australia and internationally.

Sfinco was founded on the belief that every Australian deserves to feel safe and confident in their digital life — regardless of their age, their technical background, or how much money they have.

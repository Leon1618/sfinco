Curious how AI is changing scams?

AI is changing how scams look

Scammers are starting to use the same AI tools everyone else is, to clone voices, fake videos, and write more convincing messages. It's still fairly rare in Australia, but worth knowing about.

The "it's me, I need help" call

Scammers can now clone a voice from just a few seconds of audio, sometimes taken from a video posted online. If you get a call that sounds exactly like a family member urgently asking for money, hang up and call them back on the number you already have for them.

Investments "endorsed" by celebrities

Fake videos of well-known news presenters, politicians, or business figures promoting a secret trading platform are increasingly convincing. Real public figures don't make money by tipping you off to investments online. Watch for the conversation quickly moving to WhatsApp or Telegram.

How to protect yourself
  • Agree on a family codeword for emergencies: something only you and close family know, to check over the phone if a call ever feels urgent or off.
  • If a "family member" call feels off, try a video call back instead of just ringing the same number. Voices can be cloned, but faking a live video convincingly is much harder.
  • Before investing based on something you saw online, check the person or company on ASIC's free Professional Registers. A real financial adviser or firm will be listed there.
  • Watch for glitches in videos: mismatched lip movement, jerky facial expressions, or audio that doesn't quite line up. These are common signs of a faked video.
  • Good spelling and grammar don't prove a message is genuine any more. AI can write perfectly well too. Judge a message by what it's asking you to do, not how well it's written.
Common scam types

Almost every scam is a variation on a small number of tricks. Once you know the pattern, it's much easier to spot, whatever channel it arrives through.

  • Impersonation scams. Someone pretends to be your bank, the police, a government department, or even a friend or family member, to get you to hand over personal details. They can fake caller ID and slot a fake text into a real conversation thread.
  • Investment scams. Convincing ads and pushy sales talk promise big returns for little or no risk. Real investments don't come with guarantees, and a genuine adviser never rushes you.
  • Jobs and employment scams. A job pays very well for very little work, sometimes "hiring" on behalf of a well-known company. They may ask you to pay money upfront before you can start.
  • Products and services scams. A fake online store, fake reviews, or a "seller" who takes your money and never delivers. Some even hijack a real business's invoices and swap in their own bank details.
  • Romance scams. Someone builds an online relationship with you, sometimes over months, then invents an emergency and asks for money. They often use a stolen photo and identity.
  • Threats and extortion scams. A caller claims to be from a trusted organisation and threatens arrest, deportation, or harm unless you pay immediately, or threatens to share private photos unless you pay them.
  • Unexpected money scams. A message claims you're owed money, or have won something you never entered for, then asks for a fee or your banking details to "release" it.

Spot the scam

Scams impersonating trusted names cost Australians millions every year. One recent wave of fake celebrity investment ads alone was linked to $7.4 million in reported losses. The good news: pausing to check is usually all it takes to avoid one. A bit of practice makes that pause easier.

Your 3-step method

The same three steps work for almost any scam, whatever shape it takes. Keep them in mind as you go through the messages below.

  • Stop. Don't hand over money or personal information to anyone you're not certain about, even if they seem to be from an organisation you already know and trust.
  • Check. Could this message or call be fake? Don't click a link in it. If you want to contact the business or department yourself, look up the number independently rather than using one from the message. If you're not sure, it's fine to say no, hang up, or delete it.
  • Protect. If something feels wrong, act quickly. Contact your bank if you notice unusual activity or a scammer gets hold of your money or details, and report the scam so others can be warned too.
How scams reach you

The tricks are similar everywhere, but knowing how each one usually shows up makes it easier to catch.

Text or SMS

Often pretends to be the government, a business, or a family member with a "new number," pushing you to act fast with a link to a fake website. Never click the link. If it claims to be family, call them back on the number you already have for them.

Email

Copies real logos and disguises the sender's address to look genuine, then asks you to log in, pay into a "changed" account, or open an unexpected attachment. Check it's real by contacting the organisation yourself, never through a link in the email.

Phone calls

Scammers call pretending to be your bank, the police, or a government department, sometimes already knowing your name or address to seem convincing. If you're not sure who's calling, hang up and call back on a number you've found yourself.

Websites

Fake sites can copy a well-known brand almost perfectly, complete with fake reviews and paid ads. Search the business name plus the word "scam" before buying, and remember a high-ranking ad doesn't mean it's genuine.

Social media and apps

Scammers set up fake profiles, sometimes using a real person's photo, to approach you on social media, dating apps, or messaging apps like WhatsApp. Be wary of anyone you've never met asking to move to a private chat or asking for money.

Other free tools worth knowing
Have I Been Pwned

Check if your email address has turned up in a known data breach. Free, and one of the most trusted tools of its kind. Look for "Notify me" on the site to get a free automatic email if you're ever caught up in a future breach. Note: it only covers email addresses, not phone numbers.

haveibeenpwned.com
OAIC Notifiable Data Breaches

The Australian Government's official list of reported data breaches, so you can check if a company you use has had one.

oaic.gov.au
ReportCyber

Report a cybercrime directly to the Australian Government, separate from calling the ACSC hotline.

cyber.gov.au/report
eSafety Commissioner

Help and reporting for online harassment, image-based abuse, or other harmful content.

esafety.gov.au
ASIC's Professional Registers

Check whether a financial adviser or investment firm is actually licensed before trusting them with money.

asic.gov.au
